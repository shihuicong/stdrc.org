Momentum Model

A physics-based description of tennis strokes as momentum input, concentration, and release—emphasizing how the body reduces effective moving mass to amplify racket-head speed.

Core Idea

Strokes are not simply “pushing forward.” Power emerges when the player injects momentum through the legs and trunk, then progressively disengages proximal segments so that a larger fraction of system momentum is expressed in distal racket-head motion.

Three-Stage Structure

Stage I — Momentum Input (Ground & Trunk)

Ground reaction forces and trunk rotation provide the initial impulse and angular momentum reservoir.

Stage II — Momentum Concentration (Selective Deceleration)

Proximal assemblies partially decelerate or “exit” to reduce effective rotational inertia, enabling momentum redistribution toward the arm–racket subsystem.

Stage III — Momentum Release (Racket-Head Expression)

Under appropriate constraints—grip friction, handle traction, and curved-path centripetal loading—redistributed momentum manifests as a rapid surge in racket-head angular velocity near impact.

Observable Signatures

The model predicts measurable signatures in 240fps footage: phase lag between trunk and racket, late acceleration peaks, and compact high-speed arcs near impact.

Relationship to PRW / DDMR / IGAA

PRW can be interpreted as a kinematic manifestation of staged momentum concentration. DDMR describes how selective deceleration enables redistribution, while IGAA describes how time-varying inertia shapes the resulting angular acceleration.

PRW Forehand

A distinctive acceleration mechanism emerging from modern high-velocity tennis strokes.

Overview

The Phase-Reversal Whip (PRW) forehand is characterized by a backward-loaded racket orientation, rapid axis reversal relative to the arm, and a whip-like release of angular velocity. In the late phase, whip-generated head speed is further reinforced by centripetal loading as the racket travels on a curved path.

Kinematic Phases

Phase I — Backward Loading

The racket axis rotates backward relative to the arm while the body continues to coil.

Phase II — Axis Reversal

The racket transitions from a backward-facing to a forward-facing axis while the arm is still in early forward rotation.

Phase III — Whip Acceleration

Stored tension releases as angular velocity surges into the racket head.

Phase IV — Centripetal Gain

Curved-path motion and inward constraint generate centripetal loading that reinforces speed and impact stability.
